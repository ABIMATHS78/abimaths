# Survey design

Write a survey that people can answer quickly and honestly, and plan how to use the answers before a single response comes in.

## Before you start

Ask in one short message for anything missing: the decision the survey supports, who will answer it and how they'll be reached, how many responses are realistic, and any questions that must be included. If the person has a draft, review it rather than starting again.

## Steps

1. **Turn the goal into research questions.** Write the three to five things you need to learn. Every survey question must serve one of them; cut the ones that don't.
2. **Choose each question's type.** Closed questions for things to count, rating scales for strength of opinion, open questions for reasons. Keep open questions to two or three, as they take effort to answer and to analyse.
3. **Write each question clearly.** One idea per question, neutral wording and everyday words. Give a time frame where it matters ("in the last month"). Ask about things people have done, not what they might do one day.
4. **Write the answer options.** Cover every case without overlaps (for example 0, 1 to 2, 3 to 5, 6 or more). Balance scales, with as many positive as negative points, and label every point. Add "Don't know" or "Prefer not to say" where an honest answer might be neither. Shuffle the order of options that have no natural order.
5. **Order and trim.** Start with easy, relevant questions, and put sensitive and demographic ones near the end. Aim for under ten minutes. Use skip logic, so people only see questions that apply to them.
6. **Plan the analysis.** For each question, say what you'll report, for which groups, and what result would change the decision. If a question has no use in the analysis, cut it.
7. **Pilot it.** Ask a few people from the audience to answer while thinking aloud. Fix anything they misread or hesitate over.

## Example

Before: "How satisfied are you with our fast, friendly service?" This is leading, and it asks two questions in one.
After: "How satisfied or dissatisfied are you with the speed of our service?", with a separate question about staff, each on a labelled five-point scale from "Very dissatisfied" to "Very satisfied".

## Rules

- Avoid leading questions ("How much do you enjoy..."), double-barrelled ones ("fast and easy"), loaded words, and absolutes such as "always" and "never".
- Be honest about precision. For a simple random sample, the 95% margin of error for a percentage is at most about 1 divided by the square root of the number of responses: roughly ±10 percentage points for 100 responses, ±5 for 400 and ±3 for 1,000. Subgroups have fewer responses, so they're less precise.
- That formula assumes a random sample. For opt-in surveys it doesn't apply, because the people who chose to answer may differ from those who didn't. Say so in the results.
- Never invent response rates, benchmarks or results. Mark assumptions "to confirm".
- Collect only the personal data you need. Tell people why you're asking, how the answers will be used and stored, and whether they're anonymous. Follow the data protection laws that apply.
- Don't promise anonymity if the survey collects email addresses or other identifiers.

## Checklist before you finish

- [ ] Every question maps to a research question and has an analysis plan.
- [ ] No question is leading, double-barrelled or ambiguous.
- [ ] Answer options cover every case, without overlaps.
- [ ] A pilot is done or planned before launch.
