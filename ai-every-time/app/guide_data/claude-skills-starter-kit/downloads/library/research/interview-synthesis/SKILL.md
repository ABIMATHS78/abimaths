# Interview synthesis

Turn interview notes or transcripts into a few themes the team can trust, with clear evidence behind each one and an honest view of how far it goes.

## Before you start

You need the transcripts or notes, the research questions, and a short profile of each participant, such as their role or segment, without names. If the research questions are missing, ask for them in one short message: without them, the synthesis drifts.

## Steps

1. **Label the participants.** Give each a code (P1, P2 and so on) and note their segment. Use only the codes from here on.
2. **Pull out observations.** Go through each interview and note what people said and did: needs, problems, workarounds, feelings and telling quotes, each with its participant code. Keep what was said apart from what you think it means.
3. **Group them into themes.** Cluster observations that share an idea. Name each theme as a finding, such as "Managers rebuild the weekly report by hand", not as a topic like "Reporting".
4. **Back each theme.** Count how many participants raised it (for example, 6 of 9), and pick two or three short quotes from different people. Look for evidence against the theme too, and record it.
5. **Rate the strength.** Strong: most participants, across segments. Moderate: several participants, or mainly one segment. Weak: one or two participants, or only hints. A single vivid story is still one participant.
6. **Answer the research questions.** Link each question to the themes that answer it. Say plainly where the interviews don't answer it, and what would.
7. **Draw implications last.** Only after the evidence is laid out, suggest what the themes might mean for the product or service, labelled as interpretation.

## Output

```
**Research questions**: <list>
**Participants**: <number, segments>

### Theme 1: <finding, as a sentence>
**Strength**: <Strong / Moderate / Weak>, raised by <n> of <N> participants (<codes>)
**Evidence**: "<quote>" (P3); "<quote>" (P7)
**Against**: <conflicting evidence, or "none found">
**Implication**: <what it might mean, labelled as interpretation>

**Unanswered**: <questions the interviews didn't settle, and how to find out>
```

## Rules

- Never invent a quote, participant or count. Quotes must be word for word; if you paraphrase, say so and drop the quotation marks.
- Use counts, not percentages, for small samples: "4 of 8" is more honest than "50%".
- Interviews show what people think and do, and why. They don't measure how common something is among all users, so don't present counts as if they did.
- Protect participants. Use codes, remove names, employers and other identifying details from quotes, and stay within the consent they gave and the data protection laws that apply.
- Don't let the loudest or most recent interview dominate. Check each theme against every transcript.
- Mark any detail you couldn't check, such as a participant's segment, "to confirm".

## Checklist before you finish

- [ ] Each theme is a finding, backed by a count and quotes from different people.
- [ ] Evidence against each theme was looked for and recorded.
- [ ] Every research question is answered, or listed as unanswered.
- [ ] No quote or detail can identify a participant.
