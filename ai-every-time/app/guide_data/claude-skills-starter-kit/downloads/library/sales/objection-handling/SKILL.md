# Objection handling

Prepare honest answers to the objections a seller hears, each backed by evidence, and know when an objection is simply right.

## 1. Get what you need

If anything below is missing, ask for it in one short message. Mark smaller gaps "to confirm" and carry on.
- the product, its pricing, and who it's for;
- the objections heard, in the buyers' own words if possible;
- evidence the seller can share: case studies, data, reviews, guarantees, security documents;
- the main competitors and alternatives, including doing nothing;
- what the product genuinely doesn't do.

## 2. Group the objections

Sort them into price, timing, need, trust and risk, authority, competitor, and fit. Note which sound like real concerns and which sound like polite brush-offs.

## 3. Find the real concern

For each objection, write one or two questions that find what's behind it, such as "When you say it's expensive, what are you comparing it with?". Answering the wrong concern wastes the moment.

## 4. Build each answer

- **Acknowledge** it in one sentence, without being patronising.
- **Ask** the clarifying question.
- **Answer** with evidence: a specific fact, a customer example, a calculation, a guarantee.
- **Check**: "Does that deal with it?"

Each answer cites evidence the seller can show. If there's none, say so and suggest what to gather.

## 5. Be honest about limits

When an objection is fair, because the product lacks a feature or costs more, say so plainly and explain the trade-off. If it's a deal-breaker, the honest move is to walk away. A customer who shouldn't have bought costs more than a lost deal.

## 6. Return the playbook

```
## <Category>: "<objection in the buyer's words>"
What it might really mean: <possible concerns>
Ask first: <clarifying question>
Answer: <two to four sentences>
Evidence: <what the seller can share, or "to confirm">
If it's fair: <honest response, or when to walk away>
```

Finish with a short practice set: each objection as a buyer would say it, followed by the best reply, so the team can rehearse.

## Rules

- Never invent customer stories, figures, testimonials, awards or guarantees. Name customers only with their permission.
- No pressure tactics: no fake deadlines, no discounts that "expire" when they don't, and no playing on fear.
- Don't run down competitors. Comparisons must be accurate, fair and provable under the advertising and consumer-protection laws where the seller works.
- Don't promise features, roadmap items, or legal or security commitments unless they're confirmed in writing.
- Never share other customers' details or confidential information.
